# Failure Cases: AI Coworker for Macy's Marketing Campaign Execution

## Overview

The AI coworker automates data handoffs that M1 analysis found were "every single transfer is manual" across a 6 to 8 week campaign cycle. Automation eliminates the staleness, version chaos, and coordination friction that M1 documented at every step. But it introduces a new category of risk: AI outputs that look polished, cite real documents, and carry high confidence labels while being factually wrong. Our process design must catch these failures before they reach the nearly 30 million Star Rewards members who see Macy's campaigns.

This document identifies seven specific failure modes grounded in our actual M3 implementation (4 LLM skills, 7 deterministic automations, 3 MCP tools, 12 RAG documents) and traces each one back to the pain points documented in M1's process analysis. For each failure, we describe the business consequence for Macy's and explain how the M4 process handles it through the Evidence screen, Review actions, audit log, and human checkpoints.

## How This List Connects to Testing

These seven failure cases directly informed the 8 to 12 DeepEval test cases in our automated test suite (`tests/test_workflow.py`). Each test case exercises one or more of the failure modes below by feeding adversarial or edge-case inputs through the orchestrator chain and scoring the AI output against rubrics that check for the specific errors described here. The audit log, Evidence screen, and five Review actions (Approve, Edit, Reject, Rerun, Escalate) are the runtime mitigations that appear in the "How the process handles it" column. They exist because we designed the process around these predicted failures, not as afterthoughts.

## Failure Cases

| # | Failure case | Why it might happen | Business consequence for Macy's | How the user would notice | How our process handles it |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **RAG retrieval surfaces the wrong document** | The naive FAISS index matches on keyword overlap, not intent. If BRAND-GL-2026-001 was updated mid-quarter but the FAISS index was not rebuilt, the Compliance Pre Check skill retrieves an outdated passage. HyQ mitigates this (8/8 correct retrievals vs 5/8 for naive), but does not eliminate the risk when the underlying corpus changes between index rebuilds. M1 identified a "2 to 3 week data freshness gap" at Step 2; the same staleness risk applies to the RAG corpus. | The compliance skill cites an outdated brand rule, approves campaign copy that violates current guidelines, and the non-compliant creative reaches Star Rewards members. At best this triggers a mid-flight correction. At worst it damages brand trust with Gold and Platinum tier loyalists who represent the highest lifetime value. | The Evidence screen shows a retrieved doc whose passage content does not match the AI's stated reasoning. The doc ID is correct (BRAND-GL-2026-001) but the section text references last quarter's tagline list, not the current one. | User clicks the "Evidence" pill on the Compliance card, inspects the cited passage, recognizes the mismatch, and clicks Reject on the Review screen with a reason ("brand guidelines doc is stale, needs index rebuild"). The rejection is logged in the audit trail. After the index is rebuilt, the user clicks Rerun and the skill re-fires against the updated corpus. |
| 2 | **LLM hallucinates a compliance finding** | Claude is asked to interpret pricing rules from PRICE-RULES-2026-001 and may invent a MAP violation that does not exist in the document (false positive), or miss a real violation documented in COMP-EX-2026-001 (false negative). The skill's deterministic helpers catch some cases (banned word scan, tagline list check), but the LLM writes the human-readable "reason" string and can fabricate reasoning that sounds authoritative. | A false positive blocks an otherwise valid campaign for 2 to 3 business days while the team investigates a non-existent violation, compressing the downstream schedule. A false negative is worse: a non-compliant "up to X percent off" claim ships without the required "starting at" minimum disclosure, exposing Macy's to FTC representative pricing enforcement. M1 noted that "up to 40% of retail promotions fail to deliver a positive ROI, often due to poor execution." Legal exposure from a pricing compliance miss falls squarely in this category. | The confidence indicator may read "High" even when the finding is hallucinated, because confidence is computed from the pass/fail pattern, not from whether the reasoning is factually grounded. The user must cross-check the Evidence screen: if the cited passage from PRICE-RULES-2026-001 does not contain the specific rule the AI invokes, that is a hallucination signal. | Every compliance finding displays its source document ID and the specific passage used (visible on both the AI Work screen and the Evidence side panel). If the cited passage does not support the AI's conclusion, the reviewer clicks Edit to correct the finding, or Reject with a reason. The edit is logged in the audit trail with the original AI version preserved alongside the human correction. For systematic hallucination patterns, the team can Escalate to CEO Vincent for a policy decision on whether to add a new RAG document or retrain the prompt. |
| 3 | **MCP tool returns stale or incomplete data** | The `check_pricing_conflicts` MCP tool validates SKU discount percentages against a hardcoded MAP-enforced brand list (Levi's, Coach, Lancome, Estee Lauder, Clinique, La Mer, Dior Beauty, Tag Heuer). If a new brand negotiates MAP protection this quarter and the list is not updated, the tool returns a false PASS. M1's data requirements document specified that "price changes propagate immediately to the GenAI layer" via webhook, but our M3 implementation uses a static product catalog (product_catalog.json, 61 SKUs). | A campaign ships a 25% discount on a newly MAP-protected prestige brand. The vendor pulls co-op funding for the quarter. Vendor co-op funding is material to promotional economics, and a single MAP violation can trigger funding clawbacks across multiple campaigns. M1 documented Macy's reliance on vendor partnerships as a core revenue lever alongside merchandise sales and Citibank credit card co-marketing. | The MCP tool output in the Evidence screen shows `status: pass` and `conflicts: []`, but a reviewer who knows the brand landscape recognizes the SKU should have been flagged. The tool's input/output JSON is fully visible on the Evidence screen. | The Evidence screen shows the exact input parameters and output of every MCP tool call. A reviewer familiar with recent vendor negotiations can spot the gap. The process mitigation: compliance helper cross-checks the MCP tool result against the BRAND-GL-2026-001 banned brand list retrieved via RAG. If the MCP tool and the RAG document disagree, the conflict surfaces in the Evidence panel. Future enhancement: automate the MAP list refresh from the pricing engine API documented in M1's data requirements. |
| 4 | **Confidence indicator misleads the reviewer** | Confidence is derived heuristically: "High" if all three compliance findings pass and RAG scores exceed 0.7; "Medium" if one finding fails; "Low" if two or more fail. This means a well-formed but factually wrong output can carry a "High" label. M1 documented the same false-confidence pattern in the manual workflow: "Files named 'final,' 'final2,' 'final_v3' with no version control" was a symptom of teams trusting the latest version without verification. The AI version of this problem is trusting the green dot. | The Campaign Manager trusts the "High confidence" label, clicks Approve without inspecting Evidence, and ships a campaign with an undetected compliance issue. The failure only surfaces when a customer or legal team flags the live creative. | The user does not notice because the confidence label discourages deeper inspection. The failure is invisible at the point of decision. | The M4 Review screen separates the Approve action from the AI Work view. The reviewer must navigate to /review, see the output, and make an explicit 5-way decision (Approve, Edit, Reject, Rerun, Escalate). This is intentionally more friction than a one-click "looks good" to combat rubber-stamping. The audit log captures who approved, when, and at what confidence level, preserving accountability. Future enhancement: flag any approval that occurs within 10 seconds of the AI output appearing, prompting a "Did you review the evidence?" confirmation. |
| 5 | **Vague campaign brief produces generic AI output** | The /start screen requires a minimum 200-character brief description and structured fields (Category, Channels, Budget, Launch Date). But a Campaign Manager can still write a technically sufficient brief that is strategically vague: "Promote beauty products for Mother's Day with a discount." All downstream AI outputs inherit this vagueness. M1 called out "Inconsistent brief formats across campaign types" and "Information gaps between strategy and execution teams" as top Step 1 pain points. | The compliance check passes (nothing to flag in vague copy), the approval brief is generic ("the campaign targets beauty buyers with a seasonal promotion"), the layout copy reads like a template. The campaign launches but underperforms because it lacks the specificity needed to resonate with Gold and Platinum tier Star Rewards members. This contributes to the "up to 40% of retail promotions deliver negative ROI" statistic M1 cited. | The AI output is technically correct and carries reasonable confidence, but the layout copy at Step 5 reads identically to last quarter's campaign. A reviewer who has seen multiple campaigns recognizes the pattern. | The /start screen's structured fields (Category, Target Launch Date, Budget, Channels) force minimum specificity. The "Load Example" presets demonstrate what a strong brief looks like. For M4 scope, the Review screen lets a reviewer Reject the brief output with a reason like "too generic, needs audience-specific hooks" and Rerun after the Campaign Manager strengthens the brief. Future enhancement: a Brief Quality skill that scores the brief before it enters the workflow and flags thin inputs. |
| 6 | **Cascade failure: bad upstream output corrupts downstream steps** | The Approval Brief Generator (Step 6b) reads the Compliance Pre Check output (Step 6a) as input. If a human Edits the compliance output on the Review screen and accidentally breaks the expected schema (e.g., removes the `recommended_action` field), the brief generator receives malformed input and produces incoherent output. M1 documented cascade risk explicitly: "Regional pricing changes after localization require restarting the entire approval process for affected variants." The AI version: a bad edit at Step 6a cascades silently through Steps 6b and 6c. | The brief generator produces nonsensical risk flags or an empty recommendation. The Revision Router at Step 6c inherits the confusion and may route the revision to the wrong owner. Time is wasted debugging Step 6b when the root cause is a schema break at Step 6a. | Step 6b's confidence indicator drops to "Low" even though Step 6a was marked "High." The mismatch between adjacent step confidence levels is a signal. The brief output may contain empty fields or obviously wrong content. | The Evidence screen's "Prior Step Outputs Referenced" section shows exactly which upstream fields were consumed. The user can navigate from Step 6b's evidence back to Step 6a and see what changed. The audit log preserves all Edit actions with timestamps, so the source of the cascade error is traceable. The Edit action on the Review screen preserves the original AI version alongside the human edit, enabling rollback. |
| 7 | **Reviewer fatigue leads to rubber-stamp approvals** | After a Campaign Manager approves 30 compliance checks in a row that all pass, they stop reading the AI output and reflexively click Approve. This is the human-in-the-loop failure mode: the gate exists in form but not in substance. M1 documented the same dynamic: "A single stakeholder's delayed response holds up the entire campaign" pushed teams toward rushed reviews to keep the 6 to 8 week cycle on track. The pressure to approve fast is a direct consequence of the timeline compression M1 identified. | AI errors slip through the human review gate undetected. The campaign ships with a compliance issue, a generic brief, or a misrouted revision. The audit log shows the pattern: same persona, multiple rapid approvals, zero Edit/Reject/Rerun actions. | The failure is not visible at the individual decision level. It emerges as a pattern in the audit log: rapid-fire approvals with no evidence inspection. | The M4 audit log captures every Review action with a timestamp. The five-action Review screen (Approve, Edit, Reject, Rerun, Escalate) is intentionally heavier than a single "Approve" button to slow the reviewer down. The Evidence pill on each AI card provides a low-friction way to glance at sources without navigating away. Future enhancement: a review quality dashboard that flags patterns of rapid approval without evidence inspection, surfacing them to the CEO persona's escalation queue. |
